    Mapping value alignment

    A distant reading of how eight AI companies define desirable conduct and potential harms, translate those definitions into training, and make them measurable through benchmarks.

    The alignment stack

    Value alignment asks how guiding principles — fairness, accountability, transparency, safety — are embedded into technical systems. We treat it as the process by which AI companies decide, together with the law and with partnerships among states, alignment research organisations and publics, how their models should behave in relation to what “humans value”.

    Research has tended to take up one part of that process at a time: red teaming, the drafting of alignment documents, the content of model cards, the actors named in them, or ethnographies of data work and training. These interventions matter more when they are joined back together, because alignment is indivisible — it runs from data production through policy documents to training, benchmarking and other forms of evaluation. We call the result a stack, in the sense that its components depend on one another.

    Introduction

    Alignment has become the main site of intervention for determining the norms and values by which AI works. As AI conditions and organises how information is consumed, alignment gives a normative orientation to how that information is generated and ordered — a pressing matter across domestic, geopolitical and public arenas alike.

    The components of the stack are three. The definition of risks, meaning what models should be prevented from reproducing — aiding criminality, breaching cybersecurity, and so on — and of virtues, meaning the conduct by which models should behave: helpfulness, faithfulness, a warm and friendly personality. The training of those risks and virtues through supervised learning, reward-based learning, adversarial learning, hard constraints. And the benchmarking of that training, where models are tested against criteria produced by companies or third parties, scored on how rigorously they reproduce a particular definition of a value or risk.

    Different kinds of training suit different kinds of values. Hard constraints matter for potentially criminal prompts; adversarial learning creates the frictions that a virtue like political diversity may require. What this map follows, then, is the operationalisation of a risk or virtue through the stack — how a definition trickles down into training and into evaluation.

    The documents of alignment

    For those who have studied content moderation, the familiar datapoint is the policy document, which stipulates what a service allows and does not allow. Alignment documents are different in kind. Companies keep policy documents of that sort, but they also produce more aspirational texts defining how models should in theory behave towards potential phenomena. This is why both we and the companies use the word “risk”: it names a potentiality that models are trained to pre-empt across a myriad of possible uses. The same holds for a virtue — a way that models should behave across a diversity of scenarios, to the point that models become norm sponsors, reproducing norms onto the world by steering users towards virtuous choices.

    Principles. First there are, for lack of a better word, canonical principles in which companies scope their alignment. As platform policies draw the finite lines of what a service can do, these chart the broad lines of an alignment project: they declare a philosophy, deciding what is good and bad and why. The documents differ in flavour by company. xAI makes clear that Grok should behave in ways antithetical to other models on the market — “truth-speaking” against values the company deems too liberal. One might read this as performative work addressed to governmental stakeholders. But it is not only that: in practical terms it is the root repository of norms, definitions, scenarios and metrics by which specific virtues and risks are trained, and the place where companies state their choices amid continuing debate about what AI is and how it will transform employment, sustainability and autonomy. There is a clear if understated way in which companies have taken up the craft of traditional political actors.

    Announcements, initiatives and grants. Then there are documents announcing projects, points of interest, partnerships and other developments not always reflected in model cards or principles. Though they can look like PR exercises, they are useful datapoints in ongoing and often shifting alignment projects — efforts to afford deliberative or public input into AI constitutions, for instance, such as Anthropic’s Collective Constitutional AI and OpenAI’s equivalent.

    Model cards. Finally there are model cards: relatively standardised reports of how a model was trained. They usually state a training objective, including the risk-mitigation strategies and virtues a company wants embedded; the training process, meaning what training was chosen, with whom and how; and the benchmarks used to verify its effectiveness. Since 2019 they have become industry practice, an effort to render machine learning more transparent in response to critiques of opaque data collection and training. They are not all exhaustive, but they state the fundamental components of the training process more fully than documents aimed at lay audiences.

    Repositories. We should not forget the world of repositories, where code contains its own forms of decision-making. For this reading we do not take them into account, though they could be combined with all of the documents above.

    Method

    We manually collected 183 documents from eight companies — American, European and Chinese — as a sample. Companies differ in how much alignment documentation they publish; Mistral, ironically, publishes almost none. We then performed a distant reading of every document using three readers: two LLMs, one frontier and one baseline, and ourselves. We call it distant reading because it is a collective reading of datapoints defined inductively. Having read a pair of documents, we determined what to collect systematically: the risk or virtue being trained, where available; the way it was trained; how it was benchmarked; and which actors were involved at each step. This was done manually for five documents per company, then given as an example to the two models. Where the models agreed on a passage, the passage was kept; where they disagreed, we made the call by verifying the document by hand.

    Extraction proceeded in two steps. First the model collected each document’s own expressions of ideal conduct, risks, training or mitigation methods, benchmarks and participating actors. Second, those expressions were categorised manually into controlled vocabularies. Conduct categories include honesty, helpfulness, harmlessness, anti-discrimination, political neutrality, factuality, reasoning, safety and knowledge; risk categories include CBRN, cybersecurity, bias, sycophancy, reward hacking, persuasion, misalignment, hallucinations, child safety and jailbreaking, among others. Where no category fits, the item remains under “Other”.

    Constancy. Constancy is not overall frequency across companies. It is a company-level measure of how consistently one company states a given virtue or risk over time: how much its definitions change from document to document, and how steadily the item appears in its corpus at all. Three things enter the score — the linguistic similarity between the definitions a company attaches to the item (45%), how much of that company’s corpus mentions it (35%), and how much of the company’s documentary timeline it spans (20%). The result is scaled by a confidence factor, so an item stated once or twice stays provisional however closely its few definitions happen to match. A unit counts as constant when it appears in at least two distinct documents and scores at least 0.45.

    The score is computed at two levels, because the two answer different questions. Each category — CBRN, child safety, political neutrality — is scored per company, and this is what ranks the rows: it is the level at which a company can be said to hold a settled position. Each item is scored separately and badged on its chip. Item names are coded finely, so one category may hold dozens of near-synonyms; near-duplicate spellings are merged, but a specific phrasing used once remains variable even where the concept behind it is constant. Read together, the two levels show a company returning to a concept steadily while naming it differently each time.

    Limitations

    The map captures published descriptions, which vary widely in scope, detail and genre. A company that publishes more will produce more source records; the weighting keeps duplicated records from inflating that further, but it cannot manufacture disclosure where none exists. Absence from a matrix therefore means absence from the collected documentation, not necessarily absence from the model-development process. Repositories, where a good deal of decision-making is encoded, are left out of this reading entirely.
